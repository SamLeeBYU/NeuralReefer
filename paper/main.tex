\begin{abstract}
Regular and systematic monitoring of coral reef habitat is an essential tool in reef management, conservation and restoration. However, traditional monitoring techniques, which involve manual collection and analysis of imagery, are spatially limited and labor-intensive. Consequently, few coral reef ecosystems are regularly and comprehensively surveyed, and most existing records suffer spatial and temporal gaps that can skew interpretation of variability and trends. Advances in marine robotics and more recently, in computer vision, offer scalable alternatives by reducing demand on human practitioners. Here, we present a modular machine learning pipeline for large-scale segmentation and classification of reef imagery, demonstrated on a case study of top-down images collected by an unmanned surface vehicle (USV) in Majuro, Marshall Islands during an active bleaching event in October 2024. We show for the first time how semantic segmentation of underwater survey imagery can be used to measure both live and bleached coral cover for different coral taxonomic groups. The approach integrates the Segment Anything Model~2 for mask proposal generation with a merging procedure tuned to balance recall and precision across coral size scales, followed by convolutional neural networks (CNNs) that filter out non-coral instances and classify coral morpho-taxonomic class and bleaching status. On a held-out test set, the pipeline attains 90.9\% accuracy, 95.8\% recall, and 94.3\% precision for mask-level coral classification. These results demonstrate the feasibility of automated coral health assessment on real-world survey data, enabling large-scale monitoring of reefs during bleaching events to study drivers of coral resilience to heatstress.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
One of the key challenges faced by stakeholders attempting to mitigate the rapid loss of coral reef ecosystems is the ability to efficiently monitor reefs, collect coral census data, and track the severity and extent of bleaching events. Coral reefs represent the most biodiverse ecosystems in our oceans— despite covering less than 1\% of the seafloor, they are home to over 25\% of all marine species \citep{reakakudla1997}. They provide ecosystem services valued upwards of trillions of dollars through fisheries, tourism, and coastal protection, and directly support the livelihoods of billions of people across the globe \citep{costanza2014,sing2022}. However, these invaluable communities are in the midst of an existential crisis. A majority of reefs are faced with local stressors like over fishing and land based sources of pollution, many regions have been impacted by large-scale outbreaks of disease, and corals all over the world are reeling from the global scale threat of coral bleaching in response to climate change \citep{burke2011,vega2025, hughes2017}. The mass bleaching of 2024 was the Fourth Global Mass Bleaching Event, affecting 84\% of all reefs on earth, and mortality in many areas approaching 100\% \cite{spady2025,birkart2025}. Coral reef stake holders— a title that applies to the 13\% of the planet's population who live near coral reefs— have deployed a wide range of local interventions to attempt to address these threats, from the establishment of MPA networks, active coral restoration efforts, the identification and cultivation of thermally tolerant corals, and more \citep{strain2019, bostrom2020}. But collecting data to guide the implementation of these tools and quantify their long term success is difficult. The scarcity of high-resolution, long-term, and spatially extensive data on patterns of coral bleaching has been a limitation to advancing our understanding of both the interaction of heat stress intensity and duration that causes coral bleaching and of environmental conditions that may promote a reef's resilience to heat stress. 

By far the most commonly used method of monitoring coral reefs, including during bleaching, is diver-led transects/phototransects \citep{rivera2025}. These surveys are extremely time and resource intensive, precluding their application at entire-reef scale and greatly limiting the temporal resolution at which surveys can be repeated \citep{cresswell2021,mcdonald2010}. Aerial surveys and satellite imagery using visible wavelength and multispectral imaging have been used to estimate live coral cover and broadscale patterns of bleaching, but do not provide colony level identification and are limited shallower reefs \citep{asner2022,ayad2025}. Robotics solutions such as autonomous underwater or surface vehicles can enable the collection of photodata of large areas of reef at high speeds \citep{raber2019}, but analyzing collected photos still requires a significant amount of time from trained experts \citep{josephitis2012}. Recent advances in machine learning technology can allow for rapid, AI-driven analysis of these photos to produce relevant coral reef health metrics such as coral cover, species distribution, and bleaching status.

Here, we present a modular machine vision pipeline for segmentation and classification of coral reef imagery. The approach uses the Segment Anything Model 2 (SAM2) foundation model to propose masks for an entire image and applies an ensemble of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to filter out non-coral instances, classify coral taxa, and assign bleaching status. 


\section{Related Work}

As machine learning techniques for image analysis have advanced, several different approaches have been applied to the task of classification of underwater coral reef imagery. Beijorn used a support vector machine model (SVM) to classify sampled points within an image, with prediction accuracy reaching 70-80\% \citep{beijbom2012}. This approach, made publicly available in the online tool CoralNet, essentially automated commonly used random or stratified point sampling methods (e.g. Coral Point Count \citep{kohlergill2006}), which can provide statistical estimates of coral cover, but do not allow for colony-level metrics that the direct area measurements of full image segmentation provides. Training of semantic segmentation models, however, requires large amounts of densely labeled data. Alonso et al. \citep{alonso2017,alonso2019} used a superpixel segmentation algorithm to augment the sparsely annotated labels generated by Coral Point Count and CoralNet into densely labeled images that can be used in training of CNN-based semantic segmentation models, and King et al. \citep{king2018} demonstrated the strength of fully convolutional neural networks for semantic segmentation of underwater coral reef imagery. Several recent studies have implemented CNNs and FCNNs for underwater coral reef imagery segmentation, with varying numbers of semantic classes, ranging from binary coral/noncoral or live/dead coral classes \citep{zhang2022, kopecky2023}, to classes for dominant coral genera and growth forms  \citep{zhang2024, li2024, lawson2025, zheng2025}, and training on photos collected during video and phototransect surveys and from online repositories. Marcohorn and Hung applied a CNN to classify coral photos taken from various angles retrieved from the photosharing site Flickr, and reported 88\% accuracy on picture-wise classifications of bleached vs. healthy \citep{macrohonhung2025}. Zheng et al. \citep{zheng2024} present CoralSCOP, which uses the Segment Anything Model \citep{kirillov2023segment} to generate masks and a parallel semantic branch to assign labels that can be defined by the user with additional training data. Here, we present 1) a novel pipeline that uses a similar parallel segmentation-labelling approach, combining Segment Anything 2 to generate masks with an ensemble of CNN submodels to assign mask labels and 2) the first application of a semantic segmentation approach to automate classification of colony scale bleaching from underwater coral reef imagery, with 8 classes of coral taxa and separate bleaching classes for each class.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Data}

Our analysis draws on coral reef imagery collected by the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution’s Yellowfin Surfzone ASV, an autonomous surface vehicle designed for shallow-water, top-down oceanographic surveys. Yellowfin captures RGB photographs at one-second intervals, producing 4,000--13,000 images per day depending on mission parameters. Each image is paired with GPS coordinates and mission metadata such as depth and temperature, providing spatial and environmental data to coral reef researchers and conservationists. Images used in this study were collected during phototransect surveys of a large patch reef in the northwest of the Majuro Lagoon. 

Majuro’s exceptional diversity presents distinct challenges for image-based classification. Variability in turbidity and natural illumination further reduce contrast and obscure colony boundaries, making the dataset biologically complex and technically demanding; these natural artifacts complicate image interpretation even for experts, let alone at the scale of thousands of images. The continuous, high-frequency record produced by Yellowfin offers a valuable opportunity to test machine learning methods for coral segmentation and classification under heterogeneous reef conditions. Figure~\ref{fig:raw-yellowfin-examples} gives a representative sample of images captured by Yellowfin, highlighting the variation in coral cover, morpho-taxonomic class composition, and environmental conditions.

Yellowfin images from Majuro were uploaded to the online computer vision platform Roboflow \citep{roboflow2020} for what is referred to as `ground-truth' annotation. Coral experts in the Cohen Laboratory at WHOI manually outlined individual colonies with polygonal masks, assisted by automated mask proposals generated by the Segment Anything Model 2 (SAM2) \citep{kirillov2023sam}. Annotators refined and corrected these proposals to ensure accuracy. At the species level, up to 82 distinct labels were identified, reflective of the high biodiversity of the Majuro reef system. Over the course of two months, WHOI's annotation team hand-annotated 552 images for a total of 14,275 individual masks.

To prepare the data for machine learning, the raw annotations were consolidated into what the annotation team identified as eight suitable morpho-taxonomic classes: \emph{Branching Acropora}, \emph{Finger Acropora}, \emph{Finger Porites}, \emph{Knob Porites}, \emph{Mounding}, \emph{Pocillopora}, \emph{Table Acropora}, and an ``Oddball'' category for grouping uncommon morphologies. Each morpho-taxonomic class was further cross-classified by bleaching status (healthy or bleached). A separate `non-coral' class was also defined to capture negative examples such as sand, rubble, algae, or spurious segmentations, although annotators were not explicitly given instruction to create negative masks. (We address the absence of negative masks in the next section.) This consolidation reduced the parameter space for model training while preserving interpretability at the morpho-taxonomic level for reef monitoring.

Annotated images were divided into training (441 images) and test (111 images) sets. Masks were exported in COCO JSON format and programmatically converted into pixel-level binary masks with associated morpho-taxonomic and bleaching labels for downstream machine learning tasks.

We summarize the training and test data in Table~\ref{tab:mask-cover}, reporting mask (i.e. individually segmented corals) counts and average coral cover across coral morpho-taxonomic classes and bleaching statuses. The distribution is substantively imbalanced: \emph{Finger Acropora} dominates in frequency, followed by \emph{Mounding} corals and \textit{Porites}. This class imbalance poses a challenge for machine learning models, which risk being biased toward overrepresented categories and underperforming on important minority classes. We discuss how we mitigate these risks in the next section.

Live coral cover (LCC) in this study is defined operationally as the proportion of image pixels occupied by annotated live coral. This measure is inherently a two-dimensional Euclidean projection of a benthic surface that is, in reality, three-dimensional and structurally complex. (We discuss this limitation in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.) Ground-truth masks drawn by the annotation team are thus taken literally at the pixel level. In all subsequent analysis of coral cover estimation, we treat these pixel-level computations as the ``true'' coral cover, negligibly free of measurement error, against which model predictions are evaluated. Figure~\ref{fig:data_coral_cover} illustrates the resulting LCC distributions across training and test sets. The two splits are broadly similar where differences can be attributed to sampling variability.

Our annotated Yellowfin dataset is tailored to the unique characteristics of top-down imagery collected during benthic surveys. Unlike existing corpora such as CoralMask \citep{coralmask2023} and USIS10K \citep{usis10k2024}, which aggregate imagery from multiple survey protocols and camera angles (including diver-operated cameras and oblique reef views), our dataset is designed to directly complement this common type of survey imagery, ensuring that the methods developed here are scalable to future photo-transect surveys, whether collected by divers or autonomous vehicle deployments, across diverse reef environments worldwide.

Our dataset also captures an unusually high degree of complexity within individual frames. As Figures~\ref{fig:annotations} and \ref{fig:data_coral_cover} show, a single image can contain hundreds of colonies spanning multiple coral species and bleaching states. By contrast, many existing datasets simplify imagery by cropping to individual colonies or restricting attention to narrow taxonomic subsets, thereby easing annotation and classification. In preserving the natural heterogeneity of reef habitats, our dataset poses greater challenges for machine learning but also better reflects the conditions under which many monitoring systems must ultimately operate.

\subsection{Machine learning pipeline}

We introduce a modular deep learning framework for high-throughput analysis of shallow-water reef imagery collected by autonomous surface vehicles. The pipeline performs end-to-end inference, transforming raw RGB image input into ecologically stratified metrics to support coral monitoring efforts. We suggest that its modular design ensures adaptability: individual components can be optimized independently or replaced as new architectures become available.

The framework operates in four main stages. First, instance segmentation is performed with a dual-configuration Segment Anything Model v2 (henceforth referred to as SAM2), tuned simultaneously for large and small coral structures to maximize recall across relative sizes of coral colonies. Second, candidate masks are classified by an ensemble of convolutional neural networks (henceforth referred to as CNNs) into meaningful categories, including coral morpho-taxonomic class, bleaching status, and a `non-coral' class to filter out bad segmentation proposals. Third, predictions from individual CNNs are integrated through a novel convex ensemble optimization scheme that learns both model and class-specific weights, improving generalization. We show that the ensemble aggregation preserves model interpretability. Finally, retained masks are spatially reconstructed to generate image-level summaries such as live coral cover, morpho-taxonomic class prevalence, and bleaching prevalence.

An overview of the full workflow of the inference pipeline is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}, while the training pipeline is discussed in the proceeding section.

\subsubsection{SAM2-Based Segmentation}
\label{sec:sam2}

The first stage of the proposed machine vision pipeline involves instance segmentation with SAM2, a foundation model developed by Meta AI for promptable image segmentation based on a Vision Transformer (ViT) encoder-decoder architecture. Given the heterogeneity of coral structures across size scales and the variance across Yellowfin images, we adopt a two-stream configuration of SAM2, tuned conjointly to capture both large and small coral colonies. Each image is passed through both configurations in parallel, generating potentially overlapping sets of candidate mask proposals that are later reconciled through a mask consolidation algorithm.

SAM2 accepts a dense grid of spatial prompts, internally transformed into learned embeddings through a transformer encoder. These prompt embeddings are fused with high-resolution image features extracted from a pre-trained ViT-H backbone, producing a set of segmentation masks with associated confidence scores. To control mask density and granularity, we vary key hyperparameters such as the number of prompt points per side, the stability score threshold, and the predicted intersection-over-union %(IoU) threshold\footnote{IoU is a common metric used in segmentation tasks to quantify how well a predicted mask overlaps with a ground truth mask.}.
The large-object configuration uses fewer, coarser prompts to encourage conservative high-precision masks, while the small-object configuration uses finer grids and lower thresholds to increase sensitivity and recall.

To tailor the SAM2-based segmentation module for detecting coral colonies across a broad range of size scales, we implement a Monte Carlo-style tuning framework that jointly optimizes both segmentation hyperparameters and image augmentation settings. Our goal is to learn two specialized configurations: one optimized for high-precision identification of large coral structures, and another tuned to maximize recall of small, fragmented colonies.

We describe this process formally as follows. Let $\Theta_\text{SAM}$ denote the SAM2 hyperparameter space, and $\Theta_\text{aug}$ denote the space of augmentation parameters used during preprocessing of the image used for segmentation. The full parameter space is thus defined as $\Theta = \Theta_\text{SAM}^2 \times \Theta_\text{aug}$, where $\Theta_\text{SAM}^2$ indicates the parameter spaces for both the large mask proposal and small mask proposal generation methods, respectively. Each configuration $\theta \in \Theta$ is a tuple consisting of both model-level and image-level parameters. We give a brief description of each tunable parameter in \ref{tab:sam2-tuned-params}.

To evaluate candidate configurations $\theta \in \Theta$, we construct a representative validation set $\mathcal{I}$ as follows. We define the constant $k$ to indicate the number of total images sampled with stratified Monte Carlo sampling for each proposed $\theta$. Each image $i \in \mathcal{I}$ is assigned to a stratum according to its number of ground truth coral annotations, and sample counts per stratum are allocated proportionally to stratum size to ensure broad coverage across annotation densities. For our purposes, we used the empirical quintiles of the number of ground truth annotations to define the boundaries of each stratum. 
For a given configuration $\theta$, we compute the predicted mask set $\mathcal{M}_\theta^{(i)}$ for each image $i$, and evaluate performance with two image-level metrics defined at the pixel level: $\text{Accuracy}(\theta)$, the average proportion of correctly matched predicted pixels as either coral or non-coral, and $\text{Recall}(\theta)$, the fraction of ground truth live coral mask pixels that are covered by a predicted coral mask. %\footnote{

To prioritize sensitivity in mask discovery while retaining precision, we define a convex score function:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:score}
    \text{Score}(\theta) = \frac{1}{\lambda} \left((\lambda-1) \cdot \text{Recall}(\theta) + \text{Accuracy}(\theta)\right),
\end{equation}
and use this as the objective for optimization. 

Optimization is performed using \texttt{forest\_minimize} from \texttt{scikit-optimize}, which builds a nonparametric surrogate model using an Extra Trees regressor to approximate the score surface over $\Theta$. At each iteration, a new configuration $\theta$ is proposed and evaluated on $\mathcal{I}$ using the scoring function above. The best configuration is selected as $\theta^* = \arg\max_{\theta \in \Theta} \text{Score}(\theta),$ with $\theta^* = (\theta^*_\text{large}, \theta^*_\text{small}, \theta^*_\text{aug})$ corresponding to the tuned large-object and small-object SAM2 parameters, as well as image-level augmentation settings. We fix $k = 30$ and run $n = 100$ optimization calls for all experiments reported in this work, and choose $\lambda=3$ in Equation \ref{eq:score} to emphasize recall. This procedure yields high-recall mask proposals across coral size scales while preserving spatial precision, and directly informs the subsequent classification module.

To reconcile the two sets of candidate masks, we define a deterministic mask consolidation algorithm that merges predictions from the large-object and small-object SAM2 configurations into a unified segmentation set. Again, formally this can be written as, $\mathcal{M}_\text{large}$ and $\mathcal{M}_\text{small}$, which denotes the respective collections of predicted masks, and $\mathcal{M}_\text{cand} = \mathcal{M}_\text{large} \cup \mathcal{M}_\text{small}$ denotes the combined set of candidates. Each mask $m_i \in \mathcal{M}_\text{cand}$ is associated with a binary segmentation map, a predicted confidence score (approximating IoU) from the integrated SAM2 prediction, and a weight $w_i$ indicative of how `confident' our classification models are of the semantic label for the proposed masks (prediction scores from the segmentation module and classification module are weighted equally here, although future work may wish to explore more efficient or novel ways of consolidating proposal masks into the segmentation map described in Section~\ref{sec:cnn}).

The consolidation algorithm proceeds by sorting all candidate masks in descending order in terms of confidence score, weighted by $w_i$. A mask is retained if it satisfies two conditions: it exceeds a minimum area threshold $\tau_\text{area}$ and it does not overlap significantly with any previously retained mask. 
This process yields a high-recall, non-redundant mask set which serves as input to the subsequent classification models.

The proposed pipeline's second stage classifies each candidate mask $m_i \in \mathcal{M}_\text{cand}$ into one of 17 classes. As described in the Methods, these classes were derived by consolidating our hand-coded segmentations, with each class representing a cross-product of morpho-taxonomic class (eight classes) and bleaching status (binary: bleached/not bleached). An additional ``non-coral'' class accounts for false positives in segmentation.

To construct a balanced and representative classification dataset, we first extract positive instances from ground truth masks and assign labels by morpho-taxonomic class and bleaching status. Negative instances are synthetically generated using the tuned SAM2 segmentation module in the previous section, selecting predicted masks with low overlap to ground truth annotations. These synthetic ``non-coral'' examples capture natural false positives from texture or color that resemble coral but lack biological structure. The resulting dataset is represented as $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^N$, where $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W}$ denote a normalized image crop extracted around mask $m_i$, where $C$ is the number of channels ($3$ for RGB), and $H = W = 128$ denote the height and width of the resized crop (a choice of resolution made to balance computational efficiency and feature information). 

The associated label $y_i$ is a one-hot vector over the class set $\mathcal{Y}$, where $\mathcal{Y}$ contains the $K$ mutually exclusive classes.

To enhance model generalization and prevent overfitting to fixed visual patterns, we employ a data augmentation strategy parameterized by a surrogate distribution $\mathcal{A}$. This distribution is designed to emulate the natural variability inherent to mask crops produced by the SAM2 segmentation model. Specifically, each training image $x_i$ is transformed through a stochastic function $\phi \sim \mathcal{A}$, yielding an augmented crop $x_i' = \phi(x_i)$. The augmentation pipeline includes randomized geometric distortions (resized cropping, affine transformations, rotations, and flips). Importantly, $\phi$ is resampled at every epoch, yielding a fresh realization of each $x_i$ drawn from the augmentation space. This process induces a stochastic prior over the image space, and in expectation, approximates the distribution of all plausible augmented variants of each object. Such per-epoch resampling can be interpreted as injecting functional noise into the training procedure, thereby improving robustness to out-of-distribution samples and better regularizing the learned representations under covariate shift \citep{chapelle2001vicinal, zhang2018mixup, cubuk2019autoaugment}.

Finally, to mitigate class imbalance and ensure adequate representation of minority categories, we oversample each class to a minimum of $1{,}000$ training examples. In our dataset, the rarest class in our dataset comprises only $13$ instances, and the threshold of $1,000$ may be adjusted depending on the size and diversity of the available training data. For classes with insufficient samples, we perform sampling with replacement, augmenting each duplicated crop using a new realization of the stochastic transformation $\phi(\cdot) \sim \mathcal{A}$. This procedure helps balances the empirical class distribution and ensures that each synthetic instance is drawn from a diverse augmentation prior, enabling the classifier to better learn class-discriminative features under our limited data regime. Each classifier is trained on a bootstrapped sample of the training data to reduce computational burden per model while introducing controlled variability across ensemble members. This sampling variability encourages decorrelation among base learners, which is crucial for reducing the generalization error of the ensemble through variance reduction. Moreover, since each classifier is trained on a slightly different empirical distribution, the ensemble can better approximate posterior uncertainty and hedge against overfitting to specific training artifacts. Optimization is performed using the Adam optimizer, which adapts learning rates per parameter and accelerates convergence in high-dimensional settings. Each classifier is optimized using the standard categorical cross-entropy (CCE) loss, encouraging discriminative learning while enabling each submodel to serve as a weak learner in the ensemble.  In our latest generation, the training dataset comprised $N=58{,}716$ mask crops derived from 411 annotated images. Of these, 38,608 were synthetically generated ``non-coral'' negatives, while minority classes were oversampled to a minimum of 1,000 instances each. 

To further enhance generalizability, robustness to sampling variability, and predictive power, we define an ensemble of classifiers within the classification stage of the proposed pipeline. We discuss the technical details of the ensemble aggregation in the next section. For each $m = 1, \ldots, M$, where $M$ denotes the number of models in the ensemble, the image crop $x_i$ is passed through a CNN classifier with a ResNet backbone \citep{he2016deep}. We adopt a modular architecture in which the final fully connected layer of the pre-trained ResNet is replaced with a four-layer multilayer perceptron head. This head comprises alternating layers of ReLU activations (use to model complex non-linear trends in the data) and dropout (used to reduce overfitting), terminating in a linear projection to a $K$-dimensional output space where $K = 17$ denotes the number of classes. Dropout regularization is progressively diminished across layers to mitigate co-adaptation, using rates of $0.7$, $0.6$, and $0.5$ respectively. A softmax activation is applied to the output logits for probabilistic interpretation. In practice, we used a configured ResNet model with 34 layers as a backbone for each layer because it strikes a favorable tradeoff between representational capacity and computational efficiency, providing sufficient depth to capture hierarchical texture and morphology cues in coral fragments without incurring the overfitting risks or training instability often associated with deeper variants.

We summarize the classification head architecture of a single module in Table~\ref{tab:cnn-architecture}. This design allows us to preserve low-level visual representations of the pre-trained ResNet backbone while enabling task-specific adaptation in the MLP head to our problem. Batch size, learning rate, and weight decay were chosen to balance computation time and regularization.

In addition to data augmentation, resampling, and ensemble aggregation, we employ careful data partitioning to ensure robustness and generalizability of the pipeline as illustrated through Figure~\ref{fig:training_pipeline}. Our 552 annotated images are first partitioned using 80\% of the images as training data. The remaining 20\% are considered images held for testing; no part of the model ever sees these 111 images before inference. After SAM2 hyperparameter tuning, the mask proposal dataset is generated and further split into a training \emph{mask} dataset meant to exclusively train the CNN classifiers in the ensemble. The held-out dataset (consisting of $17,615$ remaining masks) is then used to train the ensemble model. These $17,615$ are further split into a training and testing set (using a 90\%/10\% training/test split) to collect the out-of-sample model performance metrics reported in the next section.

\subsubsection{Ensemble Aggregation}

To aggregate the predictions of the $M$ independently trained CNN classifiers, we construct an ensemble model that aggregates the instance-level probability vectors through a learned weighting scheme, which we establish here as a parsimonious, yet flexible interpretation of model stacking \citep{wolpert1992stacked, dietterich2000ensemble}. Here we discuss the technical details of our ensemble aggregation and present formally the implications of our results.

As defined earlier, we let $\mathcal{D} = \{(\bm{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^N$ be a dataset where $y_i \in {1, \dots, K}$ denotes the class label for feature vector $\bm{x}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}$. Consider an ensemble of $M$ pre-trained models, where the $m$-th model produces a predicted probability vector $\hat{\bm{p}}^{(m)}_i \in \Delta^{K-1}$ for input $\bm{x}_i$. To improve the ensemble’s flexibility and performance, we introduce two sets of learnable parameters. The first consists of model weights $\bm{\alpha} = (\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_M)^\intercal$, which govern the overall contribution of each model to the ensemble. The second consists of class-specific weight matrix $\bm{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{M\times K}$ for each model, and, for notation's sake, each row vector in this weight matrix is denoted as $\bm{w}_m \in \mathbb{R}^K$. This design enables the ensemble to adaptively capture strengths of individual models: if, due to stochastic variation in training, a particular model learns features of a specific class more effectively, the framework can assign higher weight to that model’s predictions for that class. In this way, the ensemble leverages class-specific expertise from individual models while maintaining overall robustness.

The final ensemble prediction $\hat{\bm{p}}_i$ is computed as a weighted aggregation of each submodel's normalized outputs. Formally,
\begin{align}
\hat{\bm{p}}_i &= \sum_{m=1}^M \alpha_m \cdot \tilde{\bm{p}}^{(m)}_i,\label{eq:aggregation} \\
\tilde{\bm{p}}^{(m)}_i &= \frac{\bm{w}_m \odot \hat{\bm{p}}^{(m)}_i}{\bm{1}^\intercal \left(\bm{w}_m \odot \hat{\bm{p}}^{(m)}_i\right)}.
\label{eq:normalization}
\end{align}
Here, $\odot$ denotes the Hadamard product and $\bm{1}$ is the all-ones vector. The normalization step in Eq.~\ref{eq:normalization} ensures that $\tilde{\bm{p}}^{(m)}_i$ remains a valid probability distribution on the simplex $\Delta^{K-1}$.

The parameters $\bm{\alpha}$ and $\bm{W} = (\bm{w}_1, \dots, \bm{w}_M)$ are optimized jointly by minimizing a weighted Categorical Cross-Entropy loss. The optimization problem can be formalized as follows.
\begin{equation} \label{eq:min_problem}
\begin{aligned}
& \underset{\bm{\alpha}, \bm{W}}{\text{minimize}}
& & \mathcal{L}(\bm{\alpha}, \bm{W}) = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{k=1}^K \nu_k \cdot \mathbbm{1}(y_i = k) \cdot \log\left( \hat{p}_{i,k} \right) \\
& \text{subject to}
& & \bm{\alpha} \in \Delta^{M-1}, \quad \bm{w}_m \in \mathbb{R}_{++}^K, \quad m = 1, \ldots, M
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where class-specific weights $\nu_k=1$ for all classes with the notable exception of the weight used for the non-coral class, which we downweight to emphasize recall of true, live coral instances. The constraints ensure numerical stability, convexity, and interpretability by requiring $\bm{\alpha}$ to lie on the probability simplex and $\bm{w}_m$ to remain strictly positive. Thus, the resulting aggregation necessarily lies in the vector space, $\Delta^{K-1}$.

The central advantage of this formulation lies in the interpretability of the learned parameters.
The simplex-constrained weights $\alpha_m$ quantify the overall responsibility of each submodel
within the ensemble, while the class-specific vectors $\bm{w}_m$ provide fine-grained calibration
of predictions across taxa. This dual weighting structure allows us to attribute ensemble decisions
both at the model level and at the class level. Figure~\ref{fig:ensemble-heatmap} visualizes this property: rows correspond to the five submodels used in the ensemble aggregation, and columns to the coral and non-coral classes. The color intensity indicates the relative responsibility of each submodel for each class, with row percentages denoting the total
share of ensemble weight $\alpha_m$. This decomposition highlights that individual models specialize in different subsets of classes (e.g., Model 5 emphasizing \emph{Finger Porites} and \emph{Table Acropora}), while the ensemble balances these contributions to achieve robust and generalizable performance.

\subsection{Coral Cover Estimation} \label{sec:reconstruct}

In the final step, we consolidate predictions into image-level summaries. Only the masks retained from the mask consolidation procedure described in Section~\ref{sec:sam2} (those satisfying spatial, confidence, and overlap constraints) are passed to the classification module. The ensemble then assigns to each candidate mask $m_i \in \mathcal{M}_\text{cand}$ a label $\hat{y}_i$, from which we reconstruct per-image statistics such as live coral cover, morpho-taxonomic class prevalence, and bleaching severity.

To estimate coral cover, we treat the union of all classified \emph{live coral} masks 
$\mathcal{M}_{\text{live}} \subseteq \mathcal{M}_\text{cand}$ as the predicted coral region (the masks in the complement of $\mathcal{M}_{\text{live}}$ being strictly non-coral instances) and calculate the percentage of pixels in each image that are included in the mask of $\mathcal{M}_{\text{live}}$. 

Analogously, ground truth coral cover is computed by taking the union of all \emph{annotated} live coral masks. 
To quantify the severity of bleaching, we compute the proportion of the area in an image that is classified as bleached. Notationally, we define $\mathcal{M}_{\text{bleached}} \subseteq \mathcal{M}_{\text{live}}$ to denote the subset of live coral masks predicted to be bleached and calculate the percentage of pixels included in the mask of $\mathcal{M}_{\text{bleached}}$.

These results support out-of-sample generalization within the Majuro domain, where acquisition geometry (top-down ASV imagery), depth, water clarity, and community composition are comparable between splits. Extrapolation beyond Majuro, or to platforms or sites with different optics, depth, turbidity, substrate mix, or coral species compositions, should be treated as a covariate-shift problem. In such cases, we recommend a lightweight site adaptation: assemble a small site-specific validation set, recompute the bias decomposition (Table~\ref{tab:bias-decomposition}), and, if systematic shifts are detected, retune SAM2 mask thresholds and recalibrate the ensemble classifier using those samples. When shifts are large (for example, markedly different image geometry or species composition), full or partial retraining on the target distribution is warranted to ensure accurate estimates of live coral cover and bleaching.

\section{Limitations \& Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

While the pipeline demonstrates strong performance in segmenting and classifying coral imagery, we discuss the obvious and perhaps not-so-obvious limitations in this section.

First, the generalizability of our results is constrained by the data domain. All images were collected by the Yellowfin Surfzone ASV over a large patch reef in the northwest of Majuro, Marshall Islands. This narrow capture space may induce distributional shift when applied to sites with different environmental conditions even across sites in Majuro. 

Second, the segmentation stage remains both the bottleneck and the largest source of potential gains. Our mask consolidation algorithm balances recall of small objects with precision of large structures, but it remains an unsupervised procedure relying on tuned hyperparameters and grid-based prompt distributions. Performance is therefore sensitive to parameterization and limits reproducibility. A promising alternative would be a hybrid approach: precede SAM2 with a lightweight supervised detector (e.g., YOLOv8), using bounding boxes as proposals and SAM2 for mask refinement. Such hybridization could mitigate false positives, reduce inference time, and provide a more supervised handle on segmentation.

Third, the computational demands of the modular pipeline pose challenges for real-time inference and scaling to large collections. The SAM2 module generates multiple mask proposals per image, taking 1--3 seconds per frame even with GPU utilization. Ensemble optimization requires repeated forward passes across CNNs, producing a concave accuracy--throughput tradeoff. Practitioners must therefore select a feasible ensemble size (e.g. with perhaps $M<5$) that balances performance with computational resources.

Fourth, the classification formulation is restrictive. We collapse a hierarchical taxonomy into a flat 17-class problem, ignoring the conditional structure that taxa and bleaching labels are only meaningful conditional on coral presence. This design risks semantically inconsistent predictions and scales poorly with taxonomy size. To illustrate the problem of dimensionality, consider $\kappa$ number of genera. The flat formulation requires an output vector in $\mathbb{R}^{2\kappa+1}$ (two bleaching states per coral taxa label, plus a non-coral class). A more parsimonious multi-task architecture would decompose outputs as $y = (G,B,C)$, where $G \in \{1,\ldots,\kappa\}$ encodes coral taxa, $B \in \{0,1\}$ bleaching, and $C \in \{0,1\}$ coral/non-coral, corresponding to an output space in $\mathbb{R}^{\kappa+2}$.%\footnote{This reduces dimensionality and respects conditional dependencies, and could be implemented with independent sigmoid heads or structured losses.} 
Advances in hierarchical and multi-label learning \citep{wehrmann2018hierarchical, deng2014large, wang2016cnn, nam2014large} suggest that architectures incorporating these dependencies improve both interpretability and model performance.

Fifth, our estimates of coral cover are computed as a Euclidean projection on the image plane, ignoring vertical relief. This limitation mirrors those of planar photographic surveys \citep{peerj954}, where estimates are biased relative to true three-dimensional rugosity. This is especially problematic for reefs with steep inclines or vertical walls, where the area available reef substrate is much greater than the 2D planar area.  %Future pipelines could incorporate stereo imaging or photogrammetry to recover depth and produce volumetric statistics. Alternatively, if researchers are concerned with estimating the \emph{distribution} of LCC unbiasedly, one extension would be to estimate and apply a weighting function to dynamically inflate/deflate predicted LCC to match more closely the actual LCC. 

Finally, our augmentation and oversampling strategy, while useful for regularization, may not capture the full variability encountered in field conditions. Regenerating augmentations per epoch does not guarantee robustness to unseen phenomena such as turbidity or glare. A more systematic characterization of the augmentation distribution relative to true environmental variability is warranted. Similarly, expanding the label space to include additional categories or finer-grained taxonomic classes would require relabeling and potential retraining of all classification modules.

In summary, the greatest opportunities for future work lie in (i) supervised or hybrid segmentation approaches, (ii) multi-task architectures that respect the hierarchical taxonomy, and (iii) ecologically informed estimators of coral cover that incorporate spatial dependence and class imbalance. These refinements would move our automated coral monitoring methodology closer to reliable, large-scale inference.

\section{Conclusion \& Discussion}

The results demonstrate that the proposed pipeline can function as a flexible and scalable framework for coral reef monitoring. Although our experiments were restricted to ASV imagery from Majuro, the pipeline is not bound to this domain. With sufficient training data, the same methodology can be applied to any collection of top-down coral images. In this respect, the proposed pipeline is less a fixed model than an adaptable architecture that can be retrained or fine-tuned for new reef systems, survey platforms, or imaging conditions.

Performance is particularly notable given the modest size of the annotated dataset. Traditional surveys rely on labor-intensive quadrat or transect analyses, whereas our approach achieves pixel-level segmentation and taxonomic classification at scale with only a few hundred labeled images annotated over the course of a couple months. The ensemble’s ability to maintain high recall across multiple taxonomic and bleaching categories highlights both the efficiency of the segmentation--classification pipeline and the effectiveness of the model's generalizability strategies.

The ensemble aggregation framework also provides a natural avenue for future extension. Because weights are estimated post hoc on out-of-sample data, submodels need not be trained on the same set of training data. This feature creates the opportunity for heterogeneous ensembles that combine classifiers trained on diverse sources, including different reef regions, imaging modalities, or even synthetic datasets. Such aggregation could enhance robustness to distributional shift and facilitate collaborative training across institutions without requiring a single unified dataset.

This work demonstrates the practicality of automated coral monitoring. By reducing dependence on manual annotation, achieving strong performance from limited training data, and introducing a modular ensemble framework, the present pipeline represents a substantive step toward scalable, general-purpose reef assessment.
