\begin{abstract}
Regular and systematic monitoring of coral reef habitat is an essential tool in reef management, conservation and restoration. However, traditional monitoring techniques, which involve manual collection and analysis of imagery, are spatially limited and labor-intensive. Consequently, few coral reef ecosystems are regularly and comprehensively surveyed, and most existing records suffer spatial and temporal gaps that can skew interpretation of variability and trends. Advances in marine robotics and more recently, in computer vision, offer scalable alternatives by reducing demand on human practitioners. Here, we present a modular machine learning pipeline for large-scale segmentation and classification of reef imagery, demonstrated on a case study of top-down images collected by an unmanned surface vehicle (USV) in Majuro, Marshall Islands during an active bleaching event in October 2024. We show for the first time how semantic segmentation of underwater survey imagery can be used to measure both live and bleached coral cover for different coral taxonomic groups. The approach integrates the Segment Anything Model~2 for mask proposal generation with a merging procedure tuned to balance recall and precision across coral size scales, followed by convolutional neural networks (CNNs) that filter out non-coral instances and classify coral morpho-taxonomic class and bleaching status. \revadd{On the held-out test set of 111 images, the full pipeline attains a live coral cover Intersection-over-Union of 0.674 and Dice coefficient of 0.781 against expert ground truth, outperforming a competing coral segmentation framework, CoralSCOP, on the same images. At the mask level, once a candidate mask has been proposed, the classification ensemble attains} 90.3\% accuracy, 94.6\% recall, and 93.0\% precision \revadd{on an out-of-sample coral morpho-taxonomic and bleaching-status classification}. These results demonstrate the feasibility of automated coral health assessment on real-world survey data, enabling large-scale monitoring of reefs during bleaching events to study drivers of coral resilience to \revdel{heatstress}\revadd{heat stress}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
One of the key challenges faced by stakeholders attempting to mitigate the rapid loss of coral reef ecosystems is the ability to efficiently monitor reefs, collect coral census data, and track the severity and extent of bleaching events. Coral reefs represent the most biodiverse ecosystems in our oceans---despite covering less than 1\% of the seafloor, they are home to over 25\% of all marine species \citep{reakakudla1997}. They provide ecosystem services valued upwards of trillions of dollars through fisheries, tourism, and coastal protection, and directly support the livelihoods of billions of people across the globe \citep{costanza2014,sing2022}. However, these invaluable communities are in the midst of an existential crisis. A majority of reefs are faced with local stressors like \revdel{over fishing}\revadd{overfishing} and \revdel{land based}\revadd{land-based} sources of pollution, many regions have been impacted by large-scale outbreaks of disease, and corals all over the world are reeling from the global scale threat of coral bleaching in response to climate change \citep{burke2011reefs,vega2025, hughes2017}. The mass bleaching of 2024 was the Fourth Global Mass Bleaching Event, affecting 84\% of all reefs on earth, and mortality in many areas approaching 100\% \cite{spady2025,birkart2025}. Coral reef \revdel{stake holders}\revadd{stakeholders}---a title that applies to the 13\% of the planet's population who live near coral reefs---have deployed a wide range of local interventions to attempt to address these threats, from the establishment of MPA networks, active coral restoration efforts, the identification and cultivation of thermally tolerant corals, and more \citep{strain2019, bostrom2020}. But collecting data to guide the implementation of these tools and quantify their \revdel{long term}\revadd{long-term} success is difficult. The scarcity of high-resolution, long-term, and spatially extensive data on patterns of coral bleaching has been a limitation to advancing our understanding of both the interaction of heat stress intensity and duration that causes coral bleaching and of environmental conditions that may promote a reef's resilience to heat stress.

By far the most commonly used method of monitoring coral reefs, including during bleaching, is diver-led transects/phototransects \citep{rivera2025}. These surveys are extremely time and resource intensive, precluding their application at entire-reef scale and greatly limiting the temporal resolution at which surveys can be repeated \citep{cresswell2021,mcdonald2010}. Aerial surveys and satellite imagery using visible wavelength and multispectral imaging have been used to estimate live coral cover and broadscale patterns of bleaching, but do not provide colony level identification and are \revdel{limited shallower reefs}\revadd{limited to shallower reefs} \citep{asner2022,ayad2025}. Robotics solutions such as autonomous underwater or surface vehicles can enable the collection of \revdel{photodata}\revadd{photo data} of large areas of reef at high speeds \citep{raber2019}, but analyzing collected photos still requires a significant amount of time from trained experts \citep{josephitis2012}. Recent advances in machine learning technology can allow for rapid, AI-driven analysis of these photos to produce relevant coral reef health metrics such as coral cover, species distribution, and bleaching status.

Here, we present a modular machine vision pipeline for segmentation and classification of coral reef imagery. The approach uses the Segment Anything Model 2 (SAM2) \revadd{\citep{ravi2024sam2}} foundation model to propose masks for an entire image and applies an ensemble of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to filter out non-coral instances, classify coral taxa, and assign bleaching status.


\section{Related Work}

As machine learning techniques for image analysis have advanced, several different approaches have been applied to the task of classification of underwater coral reef imagery. \revdel{Beijorn}\revadd{Beijbom} used a support vector machine model (SVM) to classify sampled points within an image, with prediction accuracy reaching 70-80\% \citep{beijbom2012}. This approach, made publicly available in the online tool CoralNet, essentially automated commonly used random or stratified point sampling methods (e.g. Coral Point Count \citep{kohlergill2006}), which can provide statistical estimates of coral cover, but do not allow for colony-level metrics that the direct area measurements of full image segmentation provides. Training of semantic segmentation models, however, requires large amounts of densely labeled data. Alonso et al. \citep{alonso2017,alonso2019} used a superpixel segmentation algorithm to augment the sparsely annotated labels generated by Coral Point Count and CoralNet into densely labeled images that can be used in training of CNN-based semantic segmentation models, and King et al. \citep{king2018} demonstrated the strength of fully convolutional neural networks for semantic segmentation of underwater coral reef imagery. Several recent studies have implemented CNNs and FCNNs for underwater coral reef imagery segmentation, with varying numbers of semantic classes, ranging from binary coral/noncoral or live/dead coral classes \citep{zhang2022, kopecky2023}, to classes for dominant coral genera and growth forms  \citep{zhang2024, li2024, lawson2025, zheng2025}, and training on photos collected during video and phototransect surveys and from online repositories. \revdel{Marcohorn}\revadd{Macrohon} and Hung applied a CNN to classify coral photos taken from various angles retrieved from the photosharing site Flickr, and reported 88\% accuracy on picture-wise classifications of bleached vs. healthy \citep{macrohonhung2025}. Zheng et al. \citep{zheng2024} present CoralSCOP, which uses the Segment Anything Model \citep{kirillov2023segment} to generate masks and a parallel semantic branch to assign labels that can be defined by the user with additional training data. Here, we present 1) a novel pipeline that uses a similar parallel segmentation-labelling approach, combining Segment Anything 2 to generate masks with an ensemble of CNN submodels to assign mask labels and 2) the first application of a semantic segmentation approach to automate classification of colony scale bleaching from underwater coral reef imagery, with 8 morpho-taxonomic classes, each further split into separate bleaching classes.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Data}

Our analysis draws on coral reef imagery collected by the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution's Yellowfin Surfzone ASV, an autonomous surface vehicle designed for shallow-water, top-down oceanographic surveys. Yellowfin captures RGB photographs at one-second intervals, producing 4,000--13,000 images per day depending on mission parameters. Each image is paired with GPS coordinates and mission metadata such as depth and temperature, providing spatial and environmental data to coral reef researchers and conservationists. Images used in this study were collected during phototransect surveys of a large patch reef in the northwest of the Majuro Lagoon.

Majuro's exceptional diversity presents distinct challenges for image-based classification. Variability in turbidity and natural illumination further reduce contrast and obscure colony boundaries, making the dataset biologically complex and technically demanding; these natural artifacts complicate image interpretation even for experts, let alone at the scale of thousands of images. The continuous, high-frequency record produced by Yellowfin offers a valuable opportunity to test machine learning methods for coral segmentation and classification under heterogeneous reef conditions. Figure~\ref{fig:raw-yellowfin-examples} gives a representative sample of images captured by Yellowfin, highlighting the variation in coral cover, morpho-taxonomic class composition, and environmental conditions.

Yellowfin images from Majuro were uploaded to the online computer vision platform Roboflow \citep{roboflow2020} for what is referred to as `ground-truth' annotation. Coral experts in the Cohen Laboratory at WHOI manually outlined individual colonies with polygonal masks, assisted by automated mask proposals generated by the Segment Anything Model 2 (SAM2) \citep{ravi2024sam2}. Annotators refined and corrected these proposals to ensure accuracy. At the species level, up to 82 distinct labels were identified, reflective of the high biodiversity of the Majuro reef system. Over the course of two months, WHOI's annotation team hand-annotated 552 images for a total of 14,275 individual masks.

To prepare the data for machine learning, the raw annotations were consolidated into what the annotation team identified as eight suitable morpho-taxonomic classes: \emph{Branching Acropora}, \emph{Finger Acropora}, \emph{Finger Porites}, \emph{Knob Porites}, \emph{Mounding}, \emph{Pocillopora}, \emph{Table Acropora}, and an ``Oddball'' category for grouping uncommon morphologies. Each morpho-taxonomic class was further cross-classified by bleaching status (healthy or bleached). A separate `non-coral' class was also defined to capture negative examples such as sand, rubble, algae, or spurious segmentations. \revadd{The `non-coral' class contains no hand-annotated masks at all: annotators were instructed only to outline live coral colonies, not to draw negative examples, so every `non-coral' training instance is instead synthetically constructed from SAM2 mask proposals with low overlap to any annotated coral colony (Section~\ref{sec:cnn}).} The consolidation \revadd{to seventeen classes} reduced the parameter space for model training while preserving interpretability at the morpho-taxonomic level for reef monitoring.

Annotated images were divided into training (441 images) and test (111 images) sets. Masks were exported in COCO JSON format and programmatically converted into pixel-level binary masks with associated morpho-taxonomic and bleaching labels for downstream machine learning tasks.

We summarize the training and test data in Table~\ref{tab:mask-cover}, reporting mask (i.e. individually segmented corals) counts and average coral cover across coral morpho-taxonomic classes and bleaching statuses. The distribution is substantively imbalanced: \emph{Finger Acropora} dominates in frequency, followed by \emph{Mounding} corals and \textit{Porites}. This class imbalance poses a challenge for machine learning models, which risk being biased toward overrepresented categories and underperforming on important minority classes. We discuss how we mitigate these risks in the next section.

The variance in annotations per image is substantial: some frames contain only a handful of colonies, while others include dozens. This heterogeneity alludes to the difficulty of the task. A successful model must be sensitive enough to detect dense aggregations of coral without missing colonies, yet also specific enough to avoid over-predicting coral in sparsely populated images. Balancing these objectives is critical for accurate estimation of coral cover.

Live coral cover (LCC) in this study is defined operationally as the proportion of image pixels occupied by annotated live coral, \revadd{including coral identified as bleached (bleached coral cover, or BCC, is reported separately as a subset of LCC; see Figure~\ref{fig:bcc_results})}. This measure is inherently a two-dimensional Euclidean projection of a benthic surface that is, in reality, three-dimensional and structurally complex. (We discuss this limitation in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.) Ground-truth masks drawn by the annotation team are thus taken literally at the pixel level. In all subsequent analysis of coral cover estimation, we treat these pixel-level computations as the ``true'' coral cover, negligibly free of measurement error, against which model predictions are evaluated. Figure~\ref{fig:data_coral_cover} illustrates the resulting LCC distributions across training and test sets. The two splits are broadly similar where differences can be attributed to sampling variability.

Our annotated Yellowfin dataset is tailored to the unique characteristics of top-down imagery collected during benthic surveys. Unlike existing corpora such as CoralMask \revadd{-- the training dataset introduced alongside CoralSCOP \citep{zheng2024}} and USIS10K \citep{usis10k2024}, which aggregate imagery from multiple survey protocols and camera angles (including diver-operated cameras and oblique reef views), our dataset is designed to directly complement this common type of survey imagery, ensuring that the methods developed here are scalable to future photo-transect surveys, whether collected by divers or autonomous vehicle deployments, across diverse reef environments worldwide.

Our dataset also captures an unusually high degree of complexity within individual frames. As Figures~\ref{fig:annotations} and \ref{fig:data_coral_cover} show, a single image can contain hundreds of colonies spanning multiple coral species and bleaching states. By contrast, many existing datasets simplify imagery by cropping to individual colonies or restricting attention to narrow taxonomic subsets, thereby easing annotation and classification. In preserving the natural heterogeneity of reef habitats, our dataset poses greater challenges for machine learning but also better reflects the conditions under which many monitoring systems must ultimately operate.

\subsection{Machine learning pipeline}

We introduce a modular deep learning framework for high-throughput analysis of shallow-water reef imagery collected by autonomous surface vehicles. The pipeline performs end-to-end inference, transforming raw RGB image input into ecologically stratified metrics to support coral monitoring efforts. We suggest that its modular design ensures adaptability: individual components can be optimized independently or replaced as new architectures become available.

The framework operates in four main stages. First, instance segmentation is performed with a dual-configuration Segment Anything Model v2 (henceforth referred to as SAM2), tuned simultaneously for large and small coral structures to maximize recall across relative sizes of coral colonies. Second, candidate masks are classified by an ensemble of convolutional neural networks (henceforth referred to as CNNs) into meaningful categories, including coral morpho-taxonomic class, bleaching status, and a `non-coral' class to filter out bad segmentation proposals. Third, predictions from individual CNNs are integrated through a \revdel{novel convex ensemble optimization scheme}\revadd{novel ensemble optimization scheme, fit by an Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm,} that learns both model and class-specific weights, improving generalization. We show that the ensemble aggregation preserves model interpretability. Finally, retained masks are spatially reconstructed to generate image-level summaries such as live coral cover, morpho-taxonomic class prevalence, and bleaching prevalence.

\subsubsection{SAM2-Based Segmentation}
\label{sec:sam2}

The first stage of the proposed machine vision pipeline involves instance segmentation with SAM2, a foundation model developed by Meta AI for promptable image segmentation based on a Vision Transformer (ViT) encoder-decoder architecture. Given the heterogeneity of coral structures across size scales and the variance across Yellowfin images, we adopt a two-stream configuration of SAM2, tuned conjointly to capture both large and small coral colonies. \revadd{A single, jointly-tuned SAM2 configuration, was initially explored, but found it could not simultaneously serve both size regimes well: hyperparameters tuned to reliably fragment and recover many small colonies (e.g.\ a dense prompt grid, permissive stability thresholds) over-propose small, spurious masks in scenes dominated by one or two large colonies, while hyperparameters tuned for conservative, high-precision large-colony masks miss small or fragmented colonies entirely. Since a single Majuro image can contain either regime (or both), no single configuration's optimum served both well, motivating two separately-tuned configurations reconciled downstream by mask consolidation.} Each image is passed through both configurations in parallel, generating potentially overlapping sets of candidate mask proposals that are later reconciled through a mask consolidation algorithm.

SAM2 accepts a dense grid of spatial prompts, internally transformed into learned embeddings through a transformer encoder. These prompt embeddings are fused with high-resolution image features extracted from a pre-trained ViT-H backbone, producing a set of segmentation masks with associated confidence scores. To control mask density and granularity, we vary key hyperparameters such as the number of prompt points per side, the stability score threshold, and the predicted intersection-over-union \revadd{(IoU)}.

The large-object configuration uses fewer, coarser prompts to encourage conservative high-precision masks, while the small-object configuration uses finer grids and lower thresholds to increase sensitivity and recall.

To tailor the SAM2-based segmentation module for detecting coral colonies across a broad range of size scales, we implement a Monte Carlo-style tuning framework that jointly optimizes both segmentation hyperparameters and image augmentation settings. Our goal is to learn two specialized configurations: one optimized for high-precision identification of large coral structures, and another tuned to maximize recall of small, fragmented colonies.

The proposed pipeline's second stage classifies each candidate mask\revdel{ $m_i$} into one of 17 classes. As described in the Methods, these classes were derived by consolidating our hand-coded segmentations, with each class representing a cross-product of morpho-taxonomic class (eight classes) and bleaching status (binary: bleached/not bleached). An additional ``non-coral'' class accounts for false positives in segmentation.

To construct a balanced and representative classification dataset, we first extract positive instances from ground truth masks and assign labels by morpho-taxonomic class and bleaching status. Negative instances are synthetically generated using the tuned SAM2 segmentation module in the previous section, selecting predicted masks with low overlap to ground truth annotations. These synthetic ``non-coral'' examples capture natural false positives from texture or color that resemble coral but lack biological structure. The resulting dataset is represented as $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^N$, where $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W}$ denote a normalized image crop extracted around the proposed mask, where $C$ is the number of channels ($3$ for RGB), and $H = W = 128$ denote the height and width of the resized crop (a choice of resolution made to balance computational efficiency and feature information).

\revadd{The associated label $y_i \in \{1, \ldots, K\}$ denotes the mutually exclusive class index\revdel{ of mask $m_i$}. We adopt this scalar class-index convention for $y_i$ consistently for the remainder of this section, including the ensemble aggregation and Expectation-Maximization derivations in the appendix.}

To enhance model generalization and prevent overfitting to fixed visual patterns, we employ a data augmentation strategy parameterized by a surrogate distribution $\mathcal{A}$. This distribution is designed to emulate the natural variability inherent to mask crops produced by the SAM2 segmentation model. Specifically, each training image $x_i$ is transformed through a stochastic function $\phi \sim \mathcal{A}$, yielding an augmented crop $x_i' = \phi(x_i)$. The augmentation pipeline includes randomized geometric distortions (resized cropping, affine transformations, rotations, and flips). Importantly, $\phi$ is resampled at every epoch. This process induces a stochastic prior over the image space, and in expectation, approximates the distribution of all plausible augmented variants of each object. Such per-epoch resampling can be interpreted as injecting functional noise into the training procedure, thereby improving robustness to out-of-distribution samples and better regularizing the learned representations under covariate shift \citep{chapelle2001vicinal, zhang2018mixup, cubuk2019autoaugment}.

Finally, to mitigate class imbalance and ensure adequate representation of minority categories, we oversample each class to a minimum of $1{,}000$ training examples. In our dataset, the rarest class in our dataset comprises only $13$ instances, and the threshold of $1,000$ may be adjusted depending on the size and diversity of the available training data. For classes with insufficient samples, we perform sampling with replacement, augmenting each duplicated crop using a new realization of the stochastic transformation $\phi(\cdot) \sim \mathcal{A}$. This procedure \revdel{helps balances}\revadd{helps balance} the empirical class distribution and ensures that each synthetic instance is drawn from a diverse augmentation prior, enabling the classifier to better learn class-discriminative features under our limited data regime. Each classifier is trained on a bootstrapped sample of the training data to reduce computational burden per model while introducing controlled variability across ensemble members. This sampling variability encourages decorrelation among base learners, which is crucial for reducing the generalization error of the ensemble through variance reduction. Moreover, since each classifier is trained on a slightly different empirical distribution, the ensemble can better approximate posterior uncertainty and hedge against overfitting to specific training artifacts. Optimization is performed using the Adam optimizer, which adapts learning rates per parameter and accelerates convergence in high-dimensional settings. Each classifier is optimized using the standard categorical cross-entropy (CCE) loss, encouraging discriminative learning while enabling each submodel to serve as a weak learner in the ensemble.  In our latest generation, the training dataset comprised $N=58{,}716$ mask crops derived from 411 annotated images. Of these, 38,608 were synthetically generated ``non-coral'' negatives, while minority classes were oversampled to a minimum of 1,000 instances each.

To further enhance generalizability, robustness to sampling variability, and predictive power, we define an ensemble of classifiers within the classification stage of the proposed pipeline. We discuss the technical details of the ensemble aggregation in the next section. For each $m = 1, \ldots, M$, where $M$ denotes the number of models in the ensemble, the image crop $x_i$ is passed through a CNN classifier with a ResNet backbone \citep{he2016deep}. We adopt a modular architecture in which the final fully connected layer of the pre-trained ResNet is replaced with a four-layer multilayer perceptron \revdel{head. This head}\revadd{head (MLP). This architecture is a standard transfer-learning convention, though an architecture search could be explored. The progressively narrowing MLP (512 $\to$ 256 $\to$ 128)} comprises alternating layers of ReLU activations (\revdel{use}\revadd{used} to model complex non-linear trends in the data) and dropout (used to reduce overfitting), terminating in a linear projection to a $K$-dimensional output space where $K = 17$ denotes the number of classes. Dropout regularization is progressively diminished across layers to mitigate co-adaptation, using rates of $0.7$, $0.6$, and $0.5$ respectively\revadd{, serving as a common default for adapting a pre-trained backbone to a new classification target}. A softmax activation is applied to the output logits for probabilistic interpretation. \revadd{We did not observe our results to be sensitive to this choice. \subsubsection{Ensemble Aggregation}
\label{sec:ensemble_aggregation}

To aggregate the predictions of the $M$ independently trained CNN classifiers, we construct an ensemble model that aggregates the instance-level probability vectors through a learned weighting scheme, which we establish here as a parsimonious, yet flexible interpretation of model stacking \citep{wolpert1992stacked, dietterich2000ensemble}. Here we discuss the technical details of our ensemble aggregation and present formally the implications of our results.

As defined earlier, we let \revdel{$\mathcal{D} = $}$\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^N$ be a \revadd{(disjoint, held-out; here $N=15{,}853$, distinct from and smaller than the $N=58{,}716$ mask crops used to train the CNN submodels themselves, Section~\ref{sec:cnn})} dataset where $y_i$ \revdel{$\in {1, \dots, K}$}\revadd{$\in \{1, \ldots, K\}$} denotes the class label for feature vector $x_i\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}$. Consider an ensemble of $M$ pre-trained models, where the $m$-th model produces a predicted probability vector $\hat{\bm{p}}^{(m)}_i \in \Delta^{K-1}$ for input \revdel{$\bm{x}_i$}\revadd{$x_i$}. To improve the ensemble's flexibility and performance, we introduce two sets of learnable parameters. The first consists of model weights $\bm{\alpha} = (\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_M)^\intercal$, which govern the overall contribution of each model to the ensemble. The second consists of class-specific weight matrix $\bm{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{M\times K}$ for each model, and, for notation's sake, each row vector in this weight matrix is denoted as $\bm{w}_m \in \mathbb{R}^K$. This design enables the ensemble to adaptively capture strengths of individual models: if, due to stochastic variation in training, a particular model learns features of a specific class more effectively, the framework can assign higher weight to that model's predictions for that class. In this way, the ensemble leverages class-specific expertise from individual models while maintaining overall robustness.

The final ensemble prediction $\hat{\bm{p}}_i$ is computed as a weighted aggregation of each submodel's normalized outputs. Formally,
\begin{align}
\hat{\bm{p}}_i &= \sum_{m=1}^M \alpha_m \cdot \tilde{\bm{p}}^{(m)}_i,\label{eq:aggregation} \\
\tilde{\bm{p}}^{(m)}_i &= \frac{\bm{w}_m \odot \hat{\bm{p}}^{(m)}_i}{\bm{1}^\intercal \left(\bm{w}_m \odot \hat{\bm{p}}^{(m)}_i\right)}.
\label{eq:normalization}
\end{align}
Here, $\odot$ denotes the Hadamard product and $\bm{1}$ is the all-ones vector. The normalization step in Eq.~\ref{eq:normalization} ensures that $\tilde{\bm{p}}^{(m)}_i$ remains a valid probability distribution on the simplex $\Delta^{K-1}$.

The parameters $\bm{\alpha}$ and $\bm{W} = (\bm{w}_1, \dots, \bm{w}_M)$ are optimized jointly by minimizing a weighted Categorical Cross-Entropy loss. The optimization problem can be formalized as follows.
\begin{equation} \label{eq:min_problem}
\begin{aligned}
& \underset{\bm{\alpha}, \bm{W}}{\text{minimize}}
& & \mathcal{L}(\bm{\alpha}, \bm{W}) = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{k=1}^K \nu_k \cdot \mathbbm{1}(y_i = k) \cdot \log\left( \hat{p}_{i,k} \right) \\
& \text{subject to}
& & \bm{\alpha} \in \Delta^{M-1}, \quad \bm{w}_m \in \mathbb{R}_{++}^K, \quad m = 1, \ldots, M
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where class-specific weights $\nu_k=1$ for all classes \revdel{with the notable exception of the weight used for the non-coral class, }\revadd{except the non-coral class, which is fixed a priori at $\nu_{\text{noncoral}} = 0.25$. }\revdel{which we downweight by a factor of four to emphasize recall of true, live coral instances. This value is fixed a priori, before ensemble fitting, to reflect the fact that }\revadd{Because }live coral typically occupies a minority of substrate cover on a given reef transect, which was informed through the perspective of our expert coral annotators (Section~\ref{sec:reconstruct})\revdel{ so that}\revadd{, }failing to recall a true coral mask is penalized roughly four times as heavily in the loss as incorrectly retaining a non-coral mask\revdel{, directly trading}\revadd{. This trades} a modest amount of precision for recall of live coral (Table~\ref{tab:model-performance}). The constraints ensure numerical stability, and interpretability by requiring $\bm{\alpha}$ to lie on the probability simplex and $\bm{w}_m$ to remain strictly positive. Thus, the resulting aggregation necessarily lies in the vector space, $\Delta^{K-1}$.

\subsubsection{Ensemble Parameter Estimation}
\revadd{Given the class-specific weights $\bm{W}$, the ensemble prediction $\hat{\bm{p}}_i$ is a convex combination (over $m$) of the reweighted, renormalized submodel outputs $\tilde{\bm{p}}_i^{(m)}$\footnote{Alternatively, the ensemble specification may be reparameterized through a multinomial regression objective, estimating $M \times K$ per-submodel, per-class calibration parameters that directly recalibrate each submodel's log-probabilities before a single shared softmax combines them, dropping $\bm{\alpha}$ (and the mixture it defines) entirely. Because this removes the bilinear $(\bm{\alpha}, \bm{W})$ coupling and the respective parameter space constraints, the resulting objective is concave in the parameters and admits a unique global optimum from a single solve, with no non-convex outer problem to restart over. We nonetheless adopt the class-reweighting specification presented here because, unlike this alternative, it preserves a directly interpretable decomposition into a per-model responsibility $\alpha_m$ and per-class calibration $\bm{w}_m$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ensemble-heatmap}), which we prioritize over guaranteed global optimality of the fit.}, motivating an Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm to optimize $\bm{\alpha}$ and $\bm{W}$ jointly.}

\revadd{The algorithm proceeds as follows. Because Equation~\ref{eq:aggregation} is the marginal likelihood of a finite mixture model, we introduce a latent variable $z_i \in \{1, \ldots, M\}$ for each instance $i$, indicating which submodel is treated as responsible for generating that instance's prediction, with $P(z_i = m) = \alpha_m$. Given current estimates $(\bm{\alpha}^{(t)}, \bm{W}^{(t)})$, the E-step computes each submodel's posterior responsibility $\gamma_{im}^{(t)}$ for each instance's observed label---intuitively, how much credit submodel $m$ should get for correctly (or incorrectly) predicting $y_i$ given the current parameters. The M-step then updates $(\bm{\alpha}, \bm{W})$ to increase the resulting expected complete-data log-likelihood $Q(\bm{\alpha}, \bm{W})$. Because $\alpha_m$ does not depend on $\bm{W}$, this separates into two independent subproblems: the $\bm{\alpha}$-update has a simple closed form (a $\gamma$-weighted average of responsibilities, normalized to sum to one). The $\bm{W}$-update is complicated by the normalization step in Equation~\ref{eq:normalization}, which makes $Q$ neither convex nor concave in $\bm{W}$; we therefore perform a generalized EM (GEM) step instead of an exact maximization, using a Minorize-Maximize (MM) update \citep{hunterlange2004mm} that constructs a surrogate function lower-bounding $Q$ and touching it at the current iterate. This surrogate separates additively across classes and is concave in each coordinate of $\bm{w}_m$, yielding a closed-form multiplicative update that is guaranteed not to decrease $Q$ (though it need not maximize $Q$ exactly). Because Equation~\ref{eq:normalization} is invariant to rescaling $\bm{w}_m$, this update leaves the overall scale of $\bm{w}_m$ unidentified, so we renormalize $\bm{w}_m$ after every M-step. Since the joint $(\bm{\alpha}, \bm{W})$ problem remains non-convex even though each conditional update is not, we run the full EM/MM recursion from several random initializations and retain the solution attaining the highest final weighted log-likelihood. We defer the full details of the E-step and M-step equations, the Taylor-expansion argument establishing the MM surrogate, and the resulting closed-form updates to \ref{app:em_derivation}.}

The central advantage of this formulation lies in the interpretability of the learned parameters.
The simplex-constrained weights $\alpha_m$ quantify the overall responsibility of each submodel
within the ensemble, while the class-specific vectors $\bm{w}_m$ provide fine-grained calibration
of predictions across taxa. This dual weighting structure allows us to attribute ensemble decisions
both at the model level and at the class level. Figure~\ref{fig:ensemble-heatmap} visualizes this property: rows correspond to the five submodels used in the ensemble aggregation, and columns to the coral and non-coral classes. The color intensity indicates the relative responsibility of each submodel for each class, with row percentages denoting the total
share of ensemble weight $\alpha_m$. This decomposition highlights that individual models specialize in different subsets of classes (e.g., Model 5 emphasizing \emph{Finger Porites} and \emph{Table Acropora}), while the ensemble balances these contributions to achieve robust and generalizable performance.

While all submodels show strong discriminatory power, the ensemble yields consistent improvements across accuracy, precision, and recall. The most substantial gain is observed in recall, showing the impact of the weighted loss function in prioritizing sensitivity to true coral instances. In this context, recall denotes the proportion of ground-truth coral masks that were correctly identified, while precision measures the proportion of predicted coral masks that correspond to true coral instances. \revadd{Accuracy, by contrast, is computed over the full 17-class classification task (morpho-taxonomic class $\times$ bleaching status, plus the non-coral class): a mask correctly flagged as coral but assigned the wrong genus or bleaching status counts as an error for accuracy, while it still counts as a correct positive for precision and recall. Consequently, accuracy and precision/recall are not directly comparable in magnitude, and a method can improve on one while lagging on the other. We additionally report the $F_2$ score, $F_2 = 5PR/(4P+R)$, which combines precision ($P$) and recall ($R$) while weighting recall four times as heavily as precision, consistent with the class weight $\nu_k$ in Eq.~\ref{eq:min_problem}. }\revdel{Because the $\nu_k$ weighting in Eq.~\ref{eq:min_problem} is $\nu_k = 1$ for every coral class and $\nu_{\text{noncoral}} = 0.25$ for the non-coral class alone, i.e., a coral-vs-non-coral cost ratio of $4{:}1$ favoring correct recall of coral. The $F_\beta$ score is itself the harmonic-mean objective corresponding to a Bayes-optimal decision rule under a $\beta^2{:}1$ cost ratio favoring recall over precision \citep{lipton2014fbeta}; with $\beta = 2$, this ratio is $\beta^2 = 4$, exactly matching the $4{:}1$ down-weighting of non-coral misclassification cost already built into $\nu_k$. Maximizing out-of-sample $F_2$ is therefore consistent with, rather than incidental to, minimizing the weighted loss of Eq.~\ref{eq:min_problem}: both encode the same judgment that failing to recall a true coral instance is roughly four times as costly as retaining a spurious non-coral one, so the ensemble weights that minimize Eq.~\ref{eq:min_problem} should be expected to also perform well when evaluated by $F_2$, and indeed do so empirically (Table~\ref{tab:model-performance}).}\revadd{This $4{:}1$ cost ratio aligns with the $F_\beta$ score's Bayes-optimal decision rule \citep{lipton2014fbeta}. By explicitly down-weighting the non-coral class ($\nu_{\text{noncoral}} = 0.25$ vs.\ $\nu_{\text{coral}} = 1$), both metrics identically encode the judgment that missing a true coral instance is four times as costly as retaining a spurious non-coral one, explaining the ensemble's strong empirical performance on $F_2$ (Table~\ref{tab:model-performance}).}

\subsection{Coral Cover Estimation} \label{sec:reconstruct}

In the final step, we consolidate predictions into image-level summaries. \revadd{As described in Section~\ref{sec:sam2}, every raw candidate mask proposed by SAM2 is first passed to the classification module, and only the masks the ensemble does not predict as non-coral advance to the mask consolidation procedure (those subsequently satisfying the spatial, confidence, and overlap constraints of Section~\ref{sec:sam2}).} The ensemble \revdel{then }assigns to each candidate mask\revdel{ $m_i$} a label $\hat{y}_i$\revadd{ (prior to consolidation)}, from which we reconstruct per-image statistics such as live coral cover, morpho-taxonomic class prevalence, and bleaching severity.

To estimate coral cover, we treat the union of all candidate masks classified as \emph{live coral} (i.e., every candidate mask not classified as non-coral) as the predicted coral region, and calculate live coral cover as the percentage of pixels in each image covered by this union.

Analogously, ground truth coral cover is computed by taking the union of all \emph{annotated} live coral masks.
To quantify the severity of bleaching, we compute the proportion of the area in an image that is classified as bleached: taking the subset of live coral masks predicted to be bleached, we calculate bleached coral cover as the percentage of pixels in each image covered by their union.

\section{Limitations \& Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

While the pipeline demonstrates strong performance in segmenting and classifying coral imagery, \revdel{we discuss the obvious and perhaps not-so-obvious limitations in this section.}\revadd{we outline several methodological and practical limitations of the current framework.}

First, the generalizability of our results is constrained by the data domain. All images were collected by the Yellowfin Surfzone ASV over a large patch reef in the northwest of Majuro, Marshall Islands. This narrow capture space may induce distributional shift when applied to sites with different environmental conditions even across sites in Majuro. \revadd{We do not view this as a defect of the proposed pipeline so much as a starting point: the modular design (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}) is intended to support continued retraining as annotated imagery from additional reef systems becomes available, with the SAM2 tuning, CNN submodels, and ensemble combiner each independently updatable without redesigning the pipeline. Demonstrating this multi-site extension empirically is future work, contingent on acquiring comparably annotated surveys from other reef systems.}

Second, the segmentation stage remains both the bottleneck and the largest source of potential gains. Our mask consolidation algorithm balances recall of small objects with precision of large structures, but it remains an unsupervised procedure relying on tuned hyperparameters and grid-based prompt distributions. Performance is therefore sensitive to parameterization and limits reproducibility. \revadd{The lowest-IoU cases are disproportionately scenes with sparse, degenerate coral cover---a handful of small live colonies scattered within a field of rubble and dead skeleton, visually similar enough to their surroundings that even expert annotators can disagree on their boundaries. One way to recover some of these missed instances is simply to run SAM2 with additional, independent prompt-grid configurations and merge the results, increasing the chance that at least one configuration proposes a mask over a small or camouflaged colony; however, since each additional configuration multiplies inference cost roughly linearly, this does not scale favorably to large surveys.} A promising alternative would be a hybrid approach: precede SAM2 with a lightweight supervised detector (e.g., YOLOv8), using bounding boxes as proposals and SAM2 for mask refinement. \revadd{Because such a detector is trained directly on the target objects, it remains a more promising direction for improving recall over simply adding more SAM2 passes.} Such hybridization could mitigate false positives, reduce inference time, and provide a more supervised handle on segmentation. \revadd{A related alternative, closer in spirit to CoralSCOP \citep{zheng2024}, would replace our dense grid-prompted SAM2 configuration with point prompting---querying SAM2 at a sparser set of learned or heuristically-placed points and applying our classification ensemble to the resulting masks---potentially reducing the number of redundant proposals that must be classified and consolidated per image.}

Third, the computational demands of the modular pipeline pose challenges for real-time inference and scaling to large collections. The SAM2 module generates multiple mask proposals per image, taking 1--3 seconds per frame even with GPU utilization. Ensemble optimization requires repeated forward passes across CNNs, producing a concave accuracy--throughput tradeoff. Practitioners must therefore select a feasible ensemble size (e.g. with perhaps $M<5$) that balances performance with computational resources. \revadd{A similar tradeoff governs test-time augmentation: because each CNN submodel was trained exclusively under the same stochastic augmentation used elsewhere in this pipeline, a single augmented crop is already a valid, in-distribution input at inference, but classification could in principle be stabilized further by classifying several independently augmented draws of the same mask and aggregating (e.g.\ the modal prediction, or another stacked re-learner) rather than a single draw. We use one augmented draw per mask at inference throughout this paper for computational efficiency, and leave a systematic evaluation of this accuracy-compute tradeoff to future work.}

\revadd{Fourth, our choice of ensemble combiner (Section~\ref{sec:cnn}) prioritizes interpretability over a marginally higher out-of-sample performance. Among the aggregation schemes we explored, a small neural meta-learner attained the highest out-of-sample recall and $F_2$, modestly exceeding the class-reweighting scheme adopted throughout this paper. However, its parameters admit no analogue of the class-reweighting scheme's per-model responsibility and per-class calibration decomposition. Revisiting the neural meta-learner with post hoc interpretability tools is a natural direction for future work if predictive performance is prioritized over interpretability.}

Fifth, the classification formulation is restrictive. We collapse a hierarchical taxonomy into a flat 17-class problem, ignoring the conditional structure that taxa and bleaching labels are only meaningful conditional on coral presence. This design risks semantically inconsistent predictions and scales poorly with taxonomy size. To illustrate the problem of dimensionality, consider $\kappa$ genera. The flat formulation requires an output vector in $\mathbb{R}^{2\kappa+1}$ (two bleaching states per coral taxa label, plus a non-coral class). A more parsimonious multi-task architecture would decompose outputs as $y = (G,B,S)$, where $G \in \{1,\ldots,\kappa\}$ encodes coral taxa, $B \in \{0,1\}$ bleaching, and $S \in \{0,1\}$ coral/non-coral, corresponding to an output space in $\mathbb{R}^{\kappa+2}$.%\footnote{This reduces dimensionality and respects conditional dependencies, and could be implemented with independent sigmoid heads or structured losses.}
Advances in hierarchical and multi-label learning \citep{wehrmann2018hierarchical, deng2014large, wang2016cnn, nam2014large} suggest that architectures incorporating these dependencies improve both interpretability and model performance.

Sixth, our estimates of coral cover are computed as a Euclidean projection on the image plane, ignoring vertical relief. This limitation mirrors those of planar photographic surveys \citep{peerj954}, where estimates are biased relative to true three-dimensional rugosity. This is especially problematic for reefs with steep inclines or vertical walls, where the area of available reef substrate is much greater than the 2D planar area.  %Future pipelines could incorporate stereo imaging or photogrammetry to recover depth and produce volumetric statistics. Alternatively, if researchers are concerned with estimating the \emph{distribution} of LCC unbiasedly, one extension would be to estimate and apply a weighting function to dynamically inflate/deflate predicted LCC to match more closely the actual LCC.

Finally, our augmentation and oversampling strategy, while useful for regularization, may not capture the full variability encountered in field conditions. Regenerating augmentations per epoch does not guarantee robustness to unseen phenomena such as turbidity or glare. A more systematic characterization of the augmentation distribution relative to true environmental variability is warranted. Similarly, expanding the label space to include additional categories or finer-grained taxonomic classes would require relabeling and potential retraining of all classification modules. \revadd{A related source of label noise stems from how synthetic non-coral training examples are constructed: a candidate SAM2 mask is retained as a non-coral negative whenever its overlap with any annotated coral polygon falls at or below a fixed tolerance (i.e.\ a negative mask has at most 20\% of the candidate mask's own pixels under our fitted pipeline's hyperparameters), and is discarded otherwise. Candidate masks with overlap strictly below this threshold are hard-labeled non-coral even though a nontrivial minority of their pixels are genuine live coral, which can confuse the classifier by pairing a partly-coral crop with a purely negative label. Lowering this threshold reduces this label noise but discards more, and disproportionately larger, candidate masks from the synthetic-negative training pool, so the current value reflects a deliberate bias-variance tradeoff. Characterizing this tradeoff more precisely is left to future work.}

In summary, the greatest opportunities for future work lie in (i) supervised or hybrid segmentation approaches, (ii) multi-task architectures that respect the hierarchical taxonomy, \revadd{(iii) equipping higher-performing but less interpretable ensemble combiners,} and (iv) ecologically informed estimators of coral cover that incorporate spatial dependence and class imbalance. These refinements would move our automated coral monitoring methodology closer to reliable, large-scale inference.

\section{Conclusion \& Discussion}

The results demonstrate that the proposed pipeline can function as a flexible and scalable framework for coral reef monitoring. Although our experiments were restricted to ASV imagery from Majuro, the pipeline is not bound to this domain. With sufficient training data, the same methodology can be applied to any collection of top-down coral images. In this respect, the proposed pipeline is less a fixed model than an adaptable architecture that can be retrained or fine-tuned for new reef systems, survey platforms, or imaging conditions.

Performance is particularly notable given the modest size of the annotated dataset. Traditional surveys rely on labor-intensive quadrat or transect analyses, whereas our approach achieves pixel-level segmentation and taxonomic classification at scale with only a few hundred labeled images annotated over the course of a couple months. The ensemble's ability to maintain high recall across multiple taxonomic and bleaching categories highlights both the efficiency of the segmentation--classification pipeline and the effectiveness of the model's generalizability strategies.

The ensemble aggregation framework also provides a natural avenue for future extension. Because weights are estimated post hoc on out-of-sample data, submodels need not be trained on the same set of training data. This feature creates the opportunity for heterogeneous ensembles that combine classifiers trained on diverse sources, including different reef regions, imaging modalities, or even synthetic datasets. Such aggregation could enhance robustness to distributional shift and facilitate collaborative training across institutions without requiring a single unified dataset.

This work demonstrates the practicality of automated coral monitoring. By reducing dependence on manual annotation, achieving strong performance from limited training data, and introducing a modular ensemble framework, the present pipeline represents a substantive step toward scalable, general-purpose reef assessment.

\revdel{This appendix gives the complete derivation of the Expectation-Maximization algorithm summarized in Section~\ref{sec:ensemble_aggregation}, used to jointly estimate the ensemble model weights $\bm{\alpha}$ and class-specific weight matrix $\bm{W}$ defined in Equation~\ref{eq:min_problem}.}\revadd{This appendix details the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm from Section~\ref{sec:ensemble_aggregation} used to jointly estimate $\bm{\alpha}$ and $\bm{W}$ (Equation~\ref{eq:min_problem}).}

\revdel{Since Equation~\ref{eq:aggregation} is the marginal likelihood of a finite mixture model, we introduce latent variables $z_i \in \{1, \ldots, M\}$ to indicate the submodel responsible for generating the prediction for each instance. In particular, $\log(\hat{p}_{i,k})$ can be expressed as a marginalization over the latent variable $z_i$:}\revadd{Treating Equation~\ref{eq:aggregation} as a finite mixture model, we introduce latent variables $z_i \in \{1, \ldots, M\}$ indicating the submodel responsible for instance $i$. The log-likelihood marginalizes over $z_i$:}
\begin{align}
\log(\hat{p}_{i,k}) &= \log\left( \sum_{m=1}^M P(z_i = m) P(y_i = k \mid z_i = m) \right) = \log\left( \sum_{m=1}^M \alpha_m \tilde{p}^{(m)}_{i,k} \right) \\
\text{where} \quad P(z_i = m) &= \alpha_m, \quad P(y_i = k \mid z_i = m) = \tilde{p}^{(m)}_{i,k}.
\end{align}
\revdel{Given the current parameter estimates $\bm{\alpha}^{(t)}$ and $\bm{W}^{(t)}$, the E-step computes the posterior responsibility of each submodel for each instance:}\revadd{The E-step computes the posterior responsibility $\gamma_{im}^{(t)}$ of each submodel:}
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:responsibility}
\gamma_{im}^{(t)} = P(z_i = m \mid y_i; \bm{\alpha}^{(t)}, \bm{W}^{(t)}) = \frac{\alpha_m^{(t)} \tilde{p}^{(m)}_{i,y_i}}{\sum_{m'=1}^M \alpha_{m'}^{(t)} \tilde{p}^{(m')}_{i,y_i}}.
\end{equation}
\revdel{Intuitively, $\gamma_{im}^{(t)}$ quantifies the degree to which submodel $m$ is responsible for the observed label $y_i$, given the current parameter estimates. In the M-step, we update the model weights $\bm{\alpha}$ and class-specific weights $\bm{W}$ by maximizing the expected complete-data log-likelihood:}\revadd{The M-step maximizes the expected complete-data log-likelihood:}
\begin{align}
Q(\bm{\alpha}, \bm{W}) &= \sum_{i=1}^N \nu_{y_i} \, \mathbb{E}_{z_i \mid y_i; \bm{\alpha}^{(t)}, \bm{W}^{(t)}} \left[ \log \left( P(z_i) P(y_i \mid z_i) \right) \right] \nonumber \\
&= \sum_{i=1}^N \nu_{y_i} \sum_{m=1}^M \gamma_{im}^{(t)} \left[ \log \alpha_m + \log \tilde{p}^{(m)}_{i,y_i} \right], \label{eq:q_function}
\end{align}
\revdel{where $P(z_i)$ and $P(y_i \mid z_i)$ on the right are evaluated at the parameters $(\bm{\alpha}, \bm{W})$ being optimized (not at $(\bm{\alpha}^{(t)}, \bm{W}^{(t)})$), while the expectation over $z_i$ is taken with respect to the posterior $\gamma_{im}^{(t)} = P(z_i = m \mid y_i; \bm{\alpha}^{(t)}, \bm{W}^{(t)})$ from Equation~\eqref{eq:responsibility}.
Since $\alpha_m$ does not depend on $\bm{W}$, we can separate the optimization into two independent subproblems. The update for $\bm{\alpha}$ is obtained by maximizing $Q(\bm{\alpha}, \bm{W})$ with respect to $\bm{\alpha}$ under the simplex constraint, yielding a closed-form solution:}\revadd{Because $\bm{\alpha}$ and $\bm{W}$ decouple, we optimize them independently. Maximizing $Q$ with respect to $\bm{\alpha}$ under the simplex constraint yields:}
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:alpha_update}
\alpha_m^{(t+1)} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N \nu_{y_i} \gamma_{im}^{(t)}}{\sum_{i=1}^N \nu_{y_i}}.
\end{equation}
\revdel{The update for $\bm{W}$ is more complex due to the normalization in Equation~\eqref{eq:normalization}. In general, the normalization step implies that $Q$ is not convex nor concave in $\bm{W}$. Hence, instead of maximizing $Q(\bm{\alpha}, \bm{W})$ directly, we perform a generalized EM (GEM) update by increasing $Q(\bm{\alpha}, \bm{W})$ with respect to $\bm{W}$ using a Minorize-Maximize (MM) update \citep{hunterlange2004mm}.
Following from Equation~\eqref{eq:q_function}, we define $s_i(\bm{w}_m) := \sum_{k=1}^K w_{m,k} \hat{p}^{(m)}_{i,k}$ and $n_m^{(t)} := \sum_{i=1}^N \nu_{y_i} \gamma_{im}^{(t)}$. Then, $w_m^{(t+1)}$ is updated by increasing the per-submodel objective function:}\revadd{The normalization in Equation~\eqref{eq:normalization} makes $Q$ neither convex nor concave in $\bm{W}$. We therefore perform a generalized EM (GEM) step with a Minorize-Maximize (MM) update \citep{hunterlange2004mm}. Defining $s_i(\bm{w}_m) := \sum_{k=1}^K w_{m,k} \hat{p}^{(m)}_{i,k}$ and $n_m^{(t)} := \sum_{i=1}^N \nu_{y_i} \gamma_{im}^{(t)}$, we increase the per-submodel objective:}
\begin{align}
\label{eq:w_update}
Q_m(\bm{w}_m) &= \sum_{i=1}^N \nu_{y_i} \gamma_{im}^{(t)} \log \left( \frac{w_{m,y_i} \hat{p}^{(m)}_{i,y_i}}{s_i(\bm{w}_m)} \right) \\
 &= \sum_{i=1}^N \nu_{y_i} \gamma_{im}^{(t)} \left[ \log w_{m,y_i} + \log \hat{p}^{(m)}_{i,y_i} - \log s_i(\bm{w}_m) \right] \nonumber \\
&= \sum_{i=1}^N \nu_{y_i} \gamma_{im}^{(t)} \log w_{m,y_i} - \sum_{i=1}^N \nu_{y_i} \gamma_{im}^{(t)} \log s_i(\bm{w}_m) + \text{const} \nonumber \\
\end{align}
\revdel{Since the first term is concave in $\bm{w}_m$ and the second term is convex in $\bm{w}_m$, we construct a surrogate function that minorizes $Q_m(\bm{w}_m)$ by linearizing the convex term around the current estimate $\bm{w}_m^{(t)}$. In particular, since $-\log s_i(\bm{w}_m)$ is convex, we can use the first-order Taylor expansion to obtain a linear lower bound:}\revadd{The first term is concave and the second is convex in $\bm{w}_m$. We minorize $Q_m(\bm{w}_m)$ by applying a first-order Taylor expansion to the convex term $-\log s_i(\bm{w}_m)$ at $\bm{w}_m^{(t)}$:}
\begin{align}
-\log s_i(\bm{w}_m) &\geq -\log s_i(\bm{w}_m^{(t)}) - \frac{1}{s_i(\bm{w}_m^{(t)})} \nabla s_i(\bm{w}_m^{(t)})^\intercal (\bm{w}_m - \bm{w}_m^{(t)}) \\
&= -\log s_i(\bm{w}_m^{(t)}) - \frac{1}{s_i(\bm{w}_m^{(t)})} \sum_{k=1}^K \hat{p}^{(m)}_{i,k} (w_{m,k} - w_{m,k}^{(t)}) \\
&= -\log s_i(\bm{w}_m^{(t)}) - \frac{s_i(\bm{w}_m)-s_i(\bm{w}_m^{(t)})}{s_i(\bm{w}_m^{(t)})}
\end{align}
\revdel{Substituting this bound into Equation~\eqref{eq:w_update} and dropping additive terms that do not depend on $\bm{w}_m$, we obtain a surrogate function $\widetilde{Q}_m(\bm{w}_m \mid \bm{w}_m^{(t)})$ that minorizes $Q_m(\bm{w}_m)$ at $\bm{w}_m^{(t)}$:}\revadd{Substituting this bound and dropping constants yields the surrogate $\widetilde{Q}_m(\bm{w}_m \mid \bm{w}_m^{(t)})$, which minorizes $Q_m(\bm{w}_m)$ at $\bm{w}_m^{(t)}$:}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:w_surrogate}
\widetilde{Q}_m(\bm{w}_m \mid \bm{w}_m^{(t)}) \doteq \sum_{k=1}^K \left[ n_{m,k}^{(t)} \log w_{m,k} - d_{m,k}^{(t)} \, w_{m,k} \right],
\end{equation}
where, for each class $k = 1, \ldots, K$,
\begin{align}
n_{m,k}^{(t)} &:= \sum_{i \,:\, y_i = k} \nu_{y_i} \, \gamma_{im}^{(t)}, &
d_{m,k}^{(t)} &:= \sum_{i=1}^N \nu_{y_i} \, \gamma_{im}^{(t)} \, \frac{\hat{p}^{(m)}_{i,k}}{s_i(\bm{w}_m^{(t)})}.
\end{align}
\revdel{Note that $n_{m,k}^{(t)}$ is the class-$k$-restricted analogue of $n_m^{(t)}$ above, with $\sum_{k=1}^K n_{m,k}^{(t)} = n_m^{(t)}$. Because Equation~\eqref{eq:w_surrogate} separates additively across classes and each summand $n_{m,k}^{(t)} \log w_{m,k} - d_{m,k}^{(t)} w_{m,k}$ is concave in the scalar $w_{m,k}$, it is maximized in closed form, yielding the multiplicative MM update}\revadd{Because Equation~\eqref{eq:w_surrogate} separates additively across classes and is concave in $w_{m,k}$, it admits the closed-form multiplicative update:}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:w_mm_update}
w_{m,k}^{(t+1)} = \frac{n_{m,k}^{(t)}}{d_{m,k}^{(t)}}, \qquad k = 1, \ldots, K.
\end{equation}
\revdel{Since $\widetilde{Q}_m(\cdot \mid \bm{w}_m^{(t)})$ minorizes $Q_m$ at $\bm{w}_m^{(t)}$, this update guarantees $Q_m(\bm{w}_m^{(t+1)}) \geq Q_m(\bm{w}_m^{(t)})$, and therefore constitutes a valid GEM step: the observed-data log-likelihood is non-decreasing across iterations even though $\bm{w}_m^{(t+1)}$ need not exactly maximize $Q_m$. Because Equation~\eqref{eq:normalization} is invariant to the rescaling $\bm{w}_m \mapsto c\bm{w}_m$ for any $c>0$, the overall scale of $\bm{w}_m^{(t+1)}$ is not identified by Equation~\eqref{eq:w_mm_update}; we therefore renormalize $\bm{w}_m^{(t+1)} \leftarrow \bm{w}_m^{(t+1)} / \bm{1}^\intercal \bm{w}_m^{(t+1)}$ after each M-step. In practice, we iterate Equation~\eqref{eq:w_mm_update} a small, fixed number of times per M-step rather than to convergence, which suffices for the GEM ascent guarantee to hold. Because the joint $(\bm{\alpha}, \bm{W})$ problem remains non-convex even though each conditional update is not, we run the full EM/MM recursion from several random initializations of $(\bm{\alpha}, \bm{W})$ and retain the one attaining the highest final weighted log-likelihood, monitoring convergence by the plateau of this log-likelihood across EM iterations.}\revadd{This update guarantees $Q_m(\bm{w}_m^{(t+1)}) \geq Q_m(\bm{w}_m^{(t)})$, constituting a valid GEM step. Because Equation~\eqref{eq:normalization} is scale-invariant, we renormalize $\bm{w}_m^{(t+1)} \leftarrow \bm{w}_m^{(t+1)} / \bm{1}^\intercal \bm{w}_m^{(t+1)}$ after each M-step. In practice, we iterate Equation~\eqref{eq:w_mm_update} a fixed number of times per M-step. Due to the joint non-convexity of $(\bm{\alpha}, \bm{W})$, we restart the EM algorithm from multiple random initializations and retain the solution maximizing the final weighted log-likelihood.}
